CRM for a Clinic: We Select and Implement a System for Medical Specifics, We Do Not Sell a Box

A private clinic rarely needs "just a CRM." It needs a chain that guides a patient from the first call to the follow-up visit, stores electronic records within the law, and does not lose bookings in the front-desk notebooks. We are an independent integrator, and we start not with a product but with your clinic: what profile, how many doctors, where money and patients leak today. Then we select and implement a medical information system, a CRM, or a combination of both. This is part of our approach to CRM implementation: we measure the effect before and after and build medical-data requirements into the architecture from the start.

Why a Private Clinic Needs a CRM and MIS

A CRM for a clinic is a system that gathers patients and requests into one funnel, automates booking and reminders, and helps bring people back for follow-up visits. Alongside it works the MIS, a medical information system, which handles doctor schedules, electronic medical records, the cash desk, and medical statistics. The first handles acquisition and retention, the second handles the treatment process and record-keeping.

Three signs show that a clinic is ready to implement a system. The front desk keeps bookings in a notebook or Excel and gets lost in reschedules. Patients come once and do not return, because no one reminds them of a follow-up visit. The director does not know which channel each patient came from or what a booking costs. If even one point describes you, manual record-keeping already costs more than automation.

What a Clinic Loses Without an Automation System

The main hidden loss is no-shows. Without automatic reminders, the share of patients who book and do not arrive usually stays at 15–30% of bookings. Every empty slot in the schedule is paid doctor time without revenue.

The second loss is quieter but larger. The clinic does not bring patients back for follow-up visits and check-ups, because there is no one to keep such a list by hand. Acquiring a new patient costs more than returning one who was already treated, so a forgotten base hits revenue harder than it seems. The third loss is blind marketing: without a CRM the director cannot see which advertising pays off and pays for channels at random.

CRM or MIS: The Difference for a Clinic

This is the key question, and confusion about it costs money. In short: the MIS is the medical core, the CRM is the funnel and marketing. A small clinic sometimes gets by with a cloud MIS with a built-in CRM module; a clinic network more often needs a combination of an MIS and a separate CRM.

What Functions a Clinic Needs: Booking, Records, Reminders, Loyalty

The function set for a clinic differs from a universal CRM. The baseline looks like this.

  • Online booking from the website, maps, and messengers, with real-time checks of a doctor's free slots.
  • Electronic medical records of patients with visit history, prescriptions, and documents.
  • Reminders about a visit, follow-up, and check-up via SMS and messengers, with one-click confirmation and rescheduling.
  • A loyalty program and tracking of repeat visits, to bring patients back instead of hunting for new ones.
  • Doctor and schedule tracking with room and equipment load.

Reminders give the fastest effect. Reviews show they reduce no-shows by 20–40%, and combined with online booking and confirmation by up to 60%, while patients with a digital reminder are 25% less likely to miss an appointment. In a summary of controlled studies, attendance with a reminder versus without is 78.6% against 67.8%, a gap of almost 11 percentage points.

Integrations: MIS, Labs, Telephony, Booking

A system is useful when it is connected to the rest of the clinic's environment, not standing as a separate island. We configure integrations for your profile.

  • Labs and LIS. Referrals and test results are pulled into the patient record automatically, without manually transferring forms.
  • Telephony and call tracking. A call creates a request, and the request source is recorded so you can see channel payback.
  • Online booking and a bot. A separate booking channel is handled by a patient booking chatbot, which takes requests around the clock and passes them straight into the schedule.
  • State systems. For licensed medical care, we set up the transfer of data to the national health system where the clinic's profile requires it.

152-FZ and Medical Data: What the System Must Ensure

A patient's health data is not ordinary personal data. The law classifies it as a special category of personal data, whose processing is restricted as a general rule, and it is at the same time medical confidentiality under 323-FZ. That is why a clinic's system faces more requirements than an online store's CRM. A working clinic system must ensure the following.

  • Consent for a special category. A separate patient consent for processing health data, not a general checkbox.
  • Servers in Russia. Collection and storage of Russian citizens' data only in Russian data centers, including cloud MIS and CRM.
  • Role-based access and logging. The doctor, front-desk clerk, and accountant see only their own scope, and actions on records are logged.
  • Medical confidentiality. Technical and organizational measures against outsider access to diagnoses.
  • Data-processing agreement. The integrator and vendor act as processors on the clinic's behalf, with responsibility fixed in writing.

The cost of a mistake has risen. A leak of a special category of personal data costs a legal entity up to 15 million rubles in fines, a repeat leak brings a turnover-based fine of 1–3% of annual revenue, and failing to notify Roskomnadzor of a leak costs 1 to 3 million rubles. We build these requirements into the project from the start rather than patching after an inspection.

How Implementation in a Clinic Goes

Implementation in medicine is not "switch it on and work," because the treatment process and patient data are at stake. We go through it step by step.

  1. Clinic audit. We look at the profile, booking channels, and current records, and determine what is needed: a CRM, an MIS, or a combination.
  2. System selection. We select a solution for the tasks and budget and justify the choice rather than pushing a single vendor.
  3. Setup. We build the schedule, services, access roles, consent forms, and reminders.
  4. Base migration. We carefully transfer records and patient history from notebooks, Excel, or an old system.
  5. Training. We train the front desk and doctors to work in the system, so it takes root instead of staying empty.
