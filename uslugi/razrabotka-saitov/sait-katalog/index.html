What a catalog website is and why a business needs one

A catalog website is a showcase of products or services with cards, filters, and search, but without a cart or online payment. Instead of buying, the client leaves a request, calls, or asks for a price. In English-language practice this mode is called catalog mode: the site shows the range and helps you choose, while the deal itself closes offline or through a manager (codecollab.pl, virtocommerce.com, 2026).

A showcase suits you better than a store when the final price cannot be published in advance, the range is complex, and the buying decision is made after a conversation. The business needs not an online purchase path but a convenient entry point: to show the product range, add filters and search over it, and then move the person toward a request. Such a site is cheaper and faster to build, because it has no cart, payment, or personal account.

How a catalog website differs from an online store

The difference is not in design but in the target action. A catalog helps you choose, a store closes the deal online. If you need a cart, payment acceptance, and order processing in the system, you need an online store, not a showcase.

Queries with "buy," "pay," and "place an order" are store intent, and we do not pull them in here. A showcase answers a different scenario: view the range and contact the seller.

Who a catalog website suits

A showcase without online sales suits cases where the price depends on the terms of the deal and the purchase goes through a manager (virtocommerce.com, codecollab.pl, 2026).

  • B2B and wholesale. The price depends on the contract, volume, and client status and is agreed individually, so publishing it as in retail does not pay off.
  • Services. A consultation, project, estimate, installation, or rental is sold, where a request for a quote matters more than automatic payment.
  • Showrooms and complex products. The purchase happens in the salon or at a dealer after a conversation, while the site works as an electronic showcase of the range.
  • Segments with a "price on request." Publishing the final price does not pay off or is impossible, and a request replaces the price list.

If it matters more to you to tell about the company and services than to show a product range with filters, take a look at a corporate website: it has a different focus.

What catalog website development includes

A catalog is a managed data layer, not a static list: categories, SKUs, characteristics, price groups for different buyers (virtocommerce.com, 2026). The showcase is assembled from this.

  • Product and service cards with photos, characteristics, a description, and a price or an "on request" note.
  • Categories and navigation across the range structure, clear to both people and the search engine.
  • Filters by properties (size, material, brand, purpose), so the client quickly narrows the choice.
  • Catalog search with suggestions, resilient to typos and synonyms.
  • Item comparison for complex products where the details of characteristics decide it.
  • Request forms in the card and in shared blocks: price inquiry, callback, quote request.
  • Product feed from 1C or spreadsheets, so products, prices, and stock update without manual entry.

Integration with 1C: loading products, prices, and stock

For a showcase, a one-way feed is usually enough: products, prices, and stock go from 1C to the site, and the data does not return, because there are no orders or statuses in the system. Such an exchange solves the catalog's main pain, when there are many items and updating them by hand is impossible.

By integrators' estimates, a basic one-way catalog feed costs from 40,000 RUB and takes 1–2 weeks, while a two-way exchange with orders returning to 1C runs 120,000–250,000 RUB and 3–6 weeks. For a catalog website without a cart, a two-way exchange is excessive: there is simply nothing to return to 1C, so there is no need to overpay for it.

Which CMS we build a catalog website on

We choose the engine based on catalog size, filter complexity, and the need for 1C (hated.ru, no-kode.ru, topsyde.com, 2026). For large commercial showcases with integration we more often take Bitrix, and there is a separate page about a Bitrix website about it.

The choice of engine is not a matter of taste but a consequence of the range and integrations. At the audit we look at the number of items, filter depth, and the presence of 1C, and only then name the CMS.

How development goes

We build a simple template catalog in 3–5 weeks, a standard project with a unique design in 6–10 weeks, and a catalog with 1C integration and complex logic in 8–12 weeks. The timeline depends on the number of categories, the number of products, and filter complexity.

  1. Brief and product range. We look at the range, number of items, and category structure, and agree on the filter logic.
  2. Prototype and design. We design the card, catalog, and request forms, and approve the look.
  3. Layout and assembly. We assemble the showcase on the chosen CMS, and set up search, filters, and comparison.
  4. Population and feed. We load the product range manually or set up a feed from 1C with prices and stock.
  5. Launch and handover. We test, move to the domain, and show how to manage the catalog and requests.
