Predictive Analytics: We Calculate What Will Happen and What to Prepare

Most business decisions are made on intuition and yesterday’s reports. Predictive analytics shifts the ground: the model learns from your history and forecasts demand, sales, customer churn, and equipment failures before they happen. We build such models on your data and check accuracy against past facts before you pay for production. This is part of our approach to AI implementation: metrics before and after, payment for a measured result.

What predictive analytics is in plain terms

Predictive analytics is forecasting future events from historical data using statistics and machine learning. The model finds patterns in past sales, orders, and customer behavior, then predicts what happens next: how much will be bought, which customers will leave, when a machine will stop. Regular reporting describes the past, while predictive analytics answers the question about the future and gives you time to prepare.

Forecast or answer to a question: how it differs from AI analytics

These services get confused, but their intent differs. AI analytics is a conversational assistant over data: you ask in plain language “how many leads did June bring,” and it answers from current figures. It is reactive and about the present. Predictive analytics is proactive and about the future: it does not answer a question, it builds a forecast and states the probability of an event in advance.

AI analyticsPredictive analytics
QuestionWhat has already happenedWhat will happen next

If you need answers to questions about current data rather than a forecast, your tool is AI analytics. If you need to predict the future, you are on the right page.

How we build forecasting models

A forecast is an engineering pipeline, not a single button. We go through it step by step.

  1. Data collection. We gather sales, orders, requests history, and external factors in one place, even if they are now scattered across 1C, CRM, and Excel.
  2. Features. We turn raw data into predictors: seasonality, promos, holidays, price, weather, customer behavior.
  3. Training. We select a model for the task. On tabular data with seasonality, gradient boosting works well and, per market reviews, gives a forecast error of 10–15% over a 4–8 week horizon with clean history of two years or more.
  4. Validation. We test the model on the past it has not seen (backtest) and measure the MAPE accuracy metric. If the forecast is no better than your current method, we say so honestly.
  5. Monitoring. Demand changes, so the model is retrained on fresh data while we watch for quality drift.

What data is needed and how much history

Predictive analytics works on what you already have. For a basic demand forecast, the following is enough:

  • sales or order history by item, ideally for 6–12 months and more;
  • a calendar of promos, campaigns, and price changes;
  • a catalog of goods or services;
  • external factors if they matter: season, weather, exchange rate, holidays.

For a churn forecast you need customer purchase and request history, for predictive maintenance you need repair logs and, where available, telemetry covering 2–3 years of failure events. The data does not have to be perfect: part of preparing for a forecast is exactly the cleaning and merging of sources. Churn and LTV forecasts come out more accurate when you have end-to-end analytics in place.

Industries and use cases

Predictive analytics brings value wherever there are repeating events and history.

  • Retail and distribution. Demand forecasting by item, auto-ordering, less shortage and dead stock. A Russian case of a federal retailer shows how an ML forecast cut MAPE error from 19.7% to 11.3%, shortage from 10% to 4.2%, and stock by 18%, with payback in the first quarter.
  • Manufacturing. Product demand forecasting and predictive maintenance of machines instead of emergency stops.
  • Services and subscription. Churn and load forecasting to retain customers and plan resources in advance.

Churn signals often hide in conversations, so pairing with call speech analytics gives the model extra features.

How implementation goes: from pilot to production

  1. Scope and data. We pick one task with a clear cost of error and check whether the data is enough.
  2. Pilot on history. We build the model and measure accuracy on the past it has not seen.
  3. Effect assessment. We compare the model’s forecast with your current planning method in money terms.
  4. Production. We embed the forecast into the workflow: purchasing, retention, repair planning.
  5. Support. We retrain the model and watch quality as demand changes.

How much it costs and when it pays off

The cost depends on the task, the state of the data, and the number of forecasted items, so we fix the estimate after a short audit. The reference point for a decision is simple: a pilot should cost less than an inaccurate forecast costs you over the same period, whether that is money frozen in stock, sales lost to shortage, or departed customers. We start with a pilot on one task precisely so that you see the payback on your own numbers before scaling.
